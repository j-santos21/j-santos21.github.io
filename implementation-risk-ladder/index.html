Implementation Risk Ladder

A scoring model that sets the go-live commitment at kickoff, when changing it is still cheap, instead of at week twelve, when it is not.

The problem

A single standard timeline is wrong in both directions

Most implementation orgs quote one number. Twelve weeks, ninety days, one quarter. It is a reasonable-sounding promise and it is almost never the right one for the account in front of you.

The failure is not that the number is too aggressive. It is that a fixed number carries no information about the account. A three-facility regional shipper with one TMS and clean location data finishes early, and the org spent twelve weeks of delivery capacity on something that needed eight. A global manufacturer with four hundred carriers, two ERPs mid-migration, and no documented exception process blows through week twelve and then keeps going, and the conversation about why happens in front of the executive sponsor.

Both outcomes are expensive. The second is worse, because a missed go-live date is not just a schedule event; it is the moment the customer starts recalculating whether they believe anything else you told them.

The model

Six factors, weighted by how little control you have over them

The scoring inputs are not a checklist of everything that can go wrong. They are the six things that, at kickoff, are already knowable and already predictive. Each is scored 1 to 5, where 5 is the worst case, and combined into a weighted risk score.

The weighting principle is the part worth arguing about: a factor is weighted by how little the delivery team can do about it once the project starts. Integration complexity is heavy work but it is your work, and effort moves it. Carrier enablement is not your work; it is hundreds of third parties who have no contract with you and no deadline of their own. That is why it carries the largest weight despite being conceptually simpler than the integration build.

 0.10 Competing ERP program, peak-season freeze window, no dedicated customer-side project manager. Real, but usually known in advance and schedulable around, which is what makes it the smallest weight rather than the largest.
Where these weights came from

They are judgment-derived priors, not coefficients fitted to outcome data. I am stating that plainly because the alternative, presenting invented weights as empirical, is the thing that makes portfolio models worthless. Calibration below covers how you would replace them with real ones and, more usefully, how much it would actually change the answer.

      The ladder

      Four tiers, four different promises

      The tier is not a label for a status report. It determines the date sales is allowed to put in front of the customer, the staffing the project gets, and the governance cadence. If the tier does not change those three things, tiering is decoration.

      The rule that makes it work

      The tier sets the committed date; the customer's preferred date does not. This is the entire mechanism, and it is the part that fails in practice, because overriding it is always locally rational. A deal is closing, the sponsor wants a number, and quoting Tier 1 to a Tier 4 account costs nothing today. It costs everything in month five. If the ladder can be overridden without a written exception and a named approver, it will be overridden, and you are back to quoting twelve weeks to everyone.

      Calibration

      How you would know whether this works

      The honest position on a model like this is that it is a hypothesis until it has seen outcomes. What follows is the method for testing it, run against a simulated cohort of 480 implementations so the procedure is visible. The cohort is generated, not observed. It is here to show what a validation would look like and to answer one question that does not require real data.

      In a real deployment the steps are: score every project at kickoff and freeze the score; record actual weeks to go-live; set each tier's commitment at the tier's historical 75th percentile; then check quarterly whether the tiers still separate and whether any factor has stopped carrying signal.

      The result worth reading carefully is that the ladder does not win on hit rate. Padding the standard promise to twenty-one weeks hits slightly more often. If hit rate is the metric, padding is the answer, and it takes about ten seconds of thought.

      The ladder wins on precision. It reaches roughly the same reliability while quoting nearly two weeks less on average, cutting the miss from 4.3 weeks to 2.7 and the mean error almost in half. That is the case for it: you are not buying a higher hit rate, you are buying the ability to promise a short timeline to the accounts that deserve one without lying to the accounts that do not. Padding gives up every simple account to protect the complex ones.

      The robustness question

      Since the weights are priors, the fair objection is that they are wrong. So the simulation was built with a deliberately different set of true weights than the model uses, plus a compounding interaction the model does not model at all. Under that mismatch the model's score still correlates with actual duration at 0.836, against 0.849 for the true weights. Approximately-right weights recover about 98% of the achievable ranking signal.

      That is the practical finding, and it holds outside the simulation: for a tiering decision you need the factors and their rough order of importance to be right. Precision in the third decimal of a weight buys almost nothing, which means the work belongs in choosing what to measure, not in tuning what you already measure.

      Limits

      What this is not

      Not validated

      No real implementation outcomes have been scored against it. Every number on this page comes from a simulation whose generating process I wrote. It demonstrates a method; it does not prove a result.

      Not a replacement for judgment

      A score of 3.1 and a score of 2.9 are the same account. The tier boundary is a convention for making a decision, not a discovered natural break, and a delivery lead should be able to move an account across it with a written reason.

      Not domain-proven

      The factors are built on how enterprise onboarding fails generally, and on supply chain visibility as I understand it from outside. Someone who has run these implementations would sharpen the anchors and probably add a seventh factor I cannot see.

      Not self-enforcing

      The model is the easy part. The hard part is an organization agreeing that a tier can override a sales commitment, and holding to it in the quarter where it costs a deal.

      Provenance

      Where this came from

      At Wintrust Financial I managed 75 to 100 concurrent agency relationships on a $200M premium finance portfolio, onboarding new clients onto the platform those relationships ran on. Onboarding had no standard timeline; every new agency got the same rough promise and roughly half of them took twice as long, usually for reasons that were visible on day one and simply were not being written down.

      I built a tiered onboarding framework that segmented incoming clients by operational complexity and assigned a timeline to the tier rather than to the client's preference. Ramp time went from six to eight weeks down to three, and the organization adopted the framework as its standard playbook.

      This page is that idea rebuilt for a different problem. The domain is different, the factors are different, and the stakes per project are an order of magnitude larger. The mechanism is the same one: move the argument about the date to the moment when changing the date is still free.
