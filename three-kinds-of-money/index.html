Three Kinds of Money

An IT asset and invoice reconciliation model, and the reason its headline number is smaller than it could have been.

Jason Santos  ·  Python, pandas  ·  37,903 invoice lines, 24 months, simulated data
01  /  The problem

Most organizations already know their technology spend contains errors. What they usually lack is a defensible answer to a narrower question: which of those errors is worth an analyst's afternoon.

Published telecom and IT asset audit practice puts roughly 30 to 40 percent of active services in some state of inventory misalignment, and finds that the median billing error survives 18 to 24 months before anyone catches it. Those figures are easy to turn into a large, exciting number.

The problem is that the large number is mostly fiction, because it blends three kinds of money that behave nothing alike. Money a vendor will actually credit back. Money already spent that nobody is getting back, where the only real value is stopping the bleeding forward. And book value on assets that have gone missing, where no cash moves at all.

Reporting those together as savings is the fastest way for a reconciliation program to lose credibility with finance. The first time someone in Accounting traces the number and finds depreciated laptop book value inside it, the program is over.

02  /  The model

The model performs a three-way match across four source systems that are deliberately built to disagree with each other, because that disagreement is the entire problem: purchase orders and receipts, the IT asset register, the HR roster, and vendor invoice lines.

Eight rules run against the joined data. Each exception it emits carries an owner, an evidence string written to be pasted into a vendor dispute, and an estimate of the analyst minutes required to work it to resolution.

03  /  Classification before valuation

Every exception is classified before it is valued, and the class determines how it is allowed to be counted.

CREDIT $8,554 of $32,048 at stake

A vendor billing error, provable against a purchase order or contract rate. Cash comes back, but only inside the vendor's backbilling window. Everything older is valued at zero.

RUN RATE $28,099 annualized, forward only

The charge is legitimate on its face. There is simply nothing on the other end of it any more. Sunk spend is not counted; the value is the monthly bill that stops.

EXPOSURE $44,665 net book value, not savings

An asset is unaccounted for or missing from the register. No cash moves. Reported separately and contributing zero to the savings figure, on purpose.

The backbilling window turns out to matter more than it looks. Of $32,048 in duplicate and overpriced hardware charges sitting in the ledger, only $8,554 is realistically collectible, because most of it aged out of the 180 day credit window before anyone looked.

That gap is the actual argument for running the reconciliation monthly rather than annually, and it is completely invisible if you report a single blended number.

04  /  What the run found
Exceptions 407 across 37,903 invoice lines and 1,135 registered assets
Spend touched 5.5% of $1,858,411 billed over 24 months
Expected 12mo value $36,653 77% run rate, 23% recoverable credit
Queue 142h total analyst effort to work every exception

The finding that actually changes how this work gets staffed is not in the dollar total. It is in the shape of the queue.

Worked in queue order, 63 of the 407 exceptions carry 80 percent of the value and clear in 12 hours. The other 344 take 130 hours and are worth about $7,300.

TOP 63 EXCEPTIONS$29,336  ·  12.2 hrs

80.0% of total expected value, 8.5% of the queue's hours

REMAINING 344$7,317  ·  130.0 hrs

20.0% of value, 91.4% of the hours

That is the difference between a half-day recurring task and a full-time role, and it only appears because effort was estimated alongside value. A queue sorted by dollars at stake would have put the 63 rate variance exceptions near the top. They are worth $1,483 combined and cost 36.8 hours to work, which makes them the single worst use of analyst time in the ledger. Sorting by value per analyst hour drops them to the bottom, where they belong.

The finding that is not about invoices

Device recovery at offboarding runs at 76 percent, and the residual concentrates in Finance, Tax, and Audit. That is a defect in the offboarding process, not in procurement, and no amount of better invoice matching will fix it. Worth naming, because a reconciliation model that only ever recommends more reconciliation is not being honest about what it found.

05  /  Decisions worth defending
01

Exposure is never called savings.

It is the single most common way these models overstate themselves, and the fastest way to lose the finance audience permanently.

02

One credit request per asset.

When a serial shows both a duplicate line and a rate variance, the rules merge into a single exception claiming the total overbilled amount. Two exceptions would mean two vendor disputes for one asset and the risk of claiming the same dollar twice. An assertion in the validation script enforces this.

03

Assets purchased after their holder left are not "unreturned."

They were never in that person's hands; the assignment field is just stale. Including them inflated exposure by roughly 3.5x in an earlier version. Overstated exposure is worse than no exposure figure at all.

04

A one-time hardware charge never becomes a monthly run rate.

An earlier version annualized laptop purchases into subscriptions that did not exist and produced a $61,802 headline instead of $36,653. The check that catches it is the third assertion in validate.py, and it exists because the bug was real.

05

Realization rates are explicit, not implied.

Duplicates realize at 90 percent because they are nearly impossible to argue with once evidenced. Rate variance realizes at 60 percent because it depends on the contract being unambiguous, and contracts frequently are not. Both sit as named parameters at the top of the file, meant to be argued with by whoever owns the vendor relationships.

06  /  Provenance
On the data

The data is simulated, because I do not have a real employer's procurement records to publish. What is not invented is the structure. The discrepancy types, the injection rates, and the category model are drawn from published telecom expense management and IT asset audit practice, cited below. The generator deliberately makes the four source systems disagree with one another, because that disagreement is the problem the model exists to solve.

The correct way to read this is as a demonstration of method, not as a benchmark. Numbers from a simulation are numbers from a simulation. The rules, the category split, and the triage logic are the transferable parts.

The model is deterministic and seeded. Eleven assertions check the output for double counting, inflated run rates, out-of-window credits valued above zero, and drift between the summary and the underlying ledger.

python3 generate_sources.py   # four simulated source systems
python3 reconcile.py          # exceptions.csv, summary.json, summary_by_rule.csv
python3 validate.py           # eleven assertions; exits non-zero on failure
